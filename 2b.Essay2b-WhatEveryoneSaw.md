# What Everyone Saw
## Part 2b of a Six-Essay Exploration of Hindu Dharma
**Version 0.19 (draft)**

-----

We come to this epic reading it one way, and the way arrives before we do. In it the story is a line of events, each event a problem, each problem with a right answer and a wrong one, and the people sorted by which they took. Kaikeyi was wicked. Vali was unjust. Ravana was proud. Rama was good. This is the reading we bring, not the one we build, and it is so much the default that we mistake it for the story itself.

There are other ways to read it. One asks how the whole thing came out — a concentration gathered at the edge of the world, drew the settled order toward it, and was finally undone. That is the instrument the essay before this one held up, and it took it as far as it went, marking where it went dark. This essay does not repeat it and does not lean on it.

This essay takes a third way. I think the epic supports it, and the pages that follow are the showing of why. But I am not going to claim it is the epic's own reading, because that is precisely the claim a lens has least right to make — and because the epic argues constantly. Jabali argues. Tara argues. Vibhishana argues. Kausalya argues. Rama delivers long disquisitions on dharma to anyone who will stand still. What the epic almost never does is adjudicate. It stages the arguments and declines to rule between them, and that declining is what leaves room for a reading like this one.

In this reading there is no one to convict and nothing to tally. There is a person, in a place, seeing what the place shows them, and choosing on it. No rule sits above them to be applied. They are the rule. And the first thing this way of reading asks is that you set down the one you came with — stop sorting the cast into the good and the wicked — long enough to watch them do the only thing any of them ever does, which is choose on what they can see.

It walks the same ground the last essay walked — the same road south, the same forest, the same war — but it is not looking at the same thing. The last essay watched what happened, and what the happening revealed. This one watches only the choosing: who saw what, who was offered more, and what each of them did with the offer. The events are assumed. What is examined is the seeing behind them.

Start from what that reading turns up at once, because it runs under everything that follows. No voice from the sky commands anyone here.

That has to be put carefully, because the easy version of it is false. People in this epic are bound by things outside their own assessment all the time, and bound hard. Rama goes into exile on his father's word, against his own reading of the succession and against everyone else's. He clears the Dandaka of rakshasas because sages asked him to and because he had given a vow, while Sita argues against it and he overrides her. The bindings are real and they are heavy.

But they are human words — a vow, a boon, a father's command — spoken by a person standing in the same world, inside the same partial sight, and open to being argued with. Jabali argues with exactly this one. Which is a different architecture from a story in which a man is told from outside to raise a knife over his own son, and is praised for raising it against everything his own sight would tell him. Nothing here arrives from a seat above the situation. Every act runs through a person looking at their context — including the contexts other people's words have put into it — and choosing on what they find. The whole drama is how far each one looks before they choose.

And here is the shape that drama takes, in figure after figure. The default reads the context only as far as it reads itself. Left alone, a person sees what their position shows them and no further, and chooses on that, and the choice is the ordinary one their position would produce. So the wider view, when it comes at all, comes from outside — from someone beside the chooser who raises it, names it, puts it in front of them. It arrives as an offer. And the whole of what happens next is whether the person takes it in. That is the mechanism the epic runs, over and over, and once you see it you cannot stop seeing it: a subdued reading surfaced by someone alongside, and a person taking it or letting it pass.

-----

## The offer surfaced, and taken

The core of the story opens on exactly this, and it opens on the easy case — the offer that surfaces a default and is taken.

Kaikeyi is not sitting in wait. She has loved Rama as her own; the want that will undo Ayodhya is nowhere in her, subdued past her own reach, until someone surfaces it. Manthara does. She has no power and no standing and needs none. She says a true thing — if Rama is crowned, Bharata is second forever — and in saying it she brings a dormant reading up into the light, where it can be acted on. That is the whole of Manthara's function. She does not compel Kaikeyi. She surfaces something that was already there and left to itself would have stayed down, and once it is surfaced Kaikeyi chooses on it and invokes the boons.

Watch what kind of offer this is, because the rest of the cast is measured against it. Manthara raises a *narrowing* view — she brings up the reading that serves the nearest interest, the one already latent, waiting only to be named. And it is taken at once, because a surfaced default runs with the grain of the person. Nothing in Kaikeyi has to be overcome. The subdued reading only has to be lifted into view, and it does the rest.

And Dasharatha cannot refuse her, and the reason he cannot is worth slowing down on, because our default reads it backwards. We are told, and we tell ourselves, that his word as king is the order's word, and so the promise binds absolutely — and we read his keeping of it as noble constancy. But look at what the boon actually was. It was personal: a private debt, granted for a private service, in a private moment. Kaikeyi is using a personal claim to force a public outcome, the succession of the realm. And Dasharatha, faced with that, collapses the two together — lets the private promise bind the public office, as though there were no line between them. He could have held the line. A king may owe a personal debt and still hold that the crown is not his to hand away on it. He does not draw that line; he cannot see it as a line at all, and so the personal swallows the public and the state takes the wound. He is not weak, and he is not noble. He is a man who could not tell his private debt from his public office, and we read the confusion as virtue because it is our confusion too.

Then Bharata, who is offered the throne outright — by circumstance, by his mother, by every convenience — and does not take it, and here too the default misreads. We file Bharata under brotherly love: the devoted brother who will not sit in Rama's place. But watch what he actually does, because it is the exact thing Dasharatha could not do. He holds the personal and the public apart. Personally, the throne is his for the taking. As a matter of the order, a king installed over an exiled rightful heir carries a permanent illegitimacy that will surface with time and fracture the realm. Bharata sees both, keeps them separate, and does not collapse toward either. He finds the third thing: the sandals on the throne, the regency, the seat held in trust. It is a compromise in form and something more exact underneath — the realm's legitimacy left where it was, its administration carried by someone else, and the two visibly not the same thing.

And it had to come from him. Had Rama instructed his brother to rule, Rama would have been acting as sovereign, which is the one thing the exile was supposed to have suspended. Bharata asks instead, and holds the asking open for fourteen years. Around it sit the other pieces of the same settlement: Kaikeyi pardoned, so the grievance has nowhere left to gather; Jabali refused, so the vow is not dissolved by convenience. Whether these were steps in a sequence anyone designed, or episodes that happen to sit together, the epic does not say. What can be said is that they hold together, and that what they hold apart is what Dasharatha could not tell apart.

That is not devotion, or not only devotion. It is the most careful thing anyone in the cast does with a state, and the personal reading we bring reduces it to love because love is the category we arrive holding.

He is worth setting for a moment against the counter-voice the epic raises and refuses. Jabali, later, argues Rama out of the exile on pure reason of state: the promise was your dead father's, the dead do not bind you, sentiment is foolishness, take the throne. Jabali would dissolve the personal entirely — treat it as nothing, mere feeling to be discarded whenever the state is served. And Rama rejects him, flatly. Which fixes the shape of the whole distinction: it is not that the public should win. Dasharatha collapsed the personal into the public and wrecked the state; Jabali would dissolve the personal for the public and Rama will not have it. The two of them are the errors on either side, and the thing held between them — both frames real, neither swallowing the other — is the rare and difficult middle almost no one in the story keeps.

Already, this early, the mechanism is complete and the range is visible. A subdued reading surfaced and taken with the grain. A man reading only the one thread his position gave him. A man reading past what his position offered by subordinating his personal default to the structural requirement. Nobody here is good or wicked. They are three depths of the one act, and the depths are the whole story.

## The offer surfaced, and declined

Now the forest, and the harder case begins — the wider view offered and let pass.

Shurpanakha comes to the hut with the plainest reading there is: she wants, and says so. She is refused, and presses, and is cut. Stay with her longer than the story does, because the story does not stay. What she saw was a man she wanted; what she got for saying it was a knife. No account of anyone's seeing makes that proportionate, and it is worth marking that the vocabulary tilts the instant we are careless — her wanting slides into arrogance, the maiming into a message. That tilt is not in what happened. It is the default reading arriving early, sorting her before we have looked. What happened, without the tilt, is only that a claim presumed rather than offered met a wall, and the wall was built with a cruelty the seeing does not require.

The report reaches Lanka, and with it the coldest offer in the epic — do not fight the man, he cannot be beaten in the field; take his wife; grief will do what armies cannot. And Ravana takes it, and takes it well, and this is the figure the whole essay turns on, because Ravana is offered more wider views than anyone in the story and declines every one.

He holds, at that moment, the best information any king in the tale possesses. His forces lose on the continent; his island has never been breached. He reads both facts correctly and chooses the one course that fits them: not confrontation, which he loses, but concentration — draw the one thing the man cannot bear to lose across the water, and hold it where the man cannot follow. From inside his seat this is not a villain's cruelty and not a doomed man's flailing. It is a sovereign reading his context accurately and choosing on it. And it is the default of his whole life — the reading that has always worked, surfaced again and taken again.

But the wider view is offered to him, and offered more than once. Maricha, who has met this man and been thrown into the sea by him, tells him plainly where the road ends. Vibhishana, his own brother, lays out the trajectory. The assembly counsels. The extension is placed in front of Ravana repeatedly, by the people best placed to place it — and each time he declines it, because taking it would cost him the thing he wants, and the default runs with the grain while the extension runs against it. His fault, if we even use the word, is not the wanting and not a failure to see. It is that a wider seeing was available to him, through counsel, through conversation, again and again, and he stayed with the reading that served him. The discussion was triggered. The default played out.

And in taking the narrow reading he puts into his hoard the one thing it cannot account for. Everything he has ever drawn across the water was silent — gold does not call out. A loved person behind the water is not a holding; she is a beacon. He has, by choosing the reading that always worked, given the sealed place its first address. He read his context correctly and to exactly the depth his want permitted, and the depth his want permitted did not reach the flaw. That is not a man failing to see. It is what choosing at the default depth looks like when the default runs out.

## The holding that will not be held

The beacon is worth staying with, because the person inside it shows the reading its edge — and shows it twice, at two depths.

The essay before this one followed the wealth reading: a concentration gathered at the edge of the world, drew the order toward it, and was undone. Sita is where that reading is applied to a person, and the whole society in the story applies it. She is not fought for the way a rival is fought; she is taken, the way a holding is taken, removed and sealed in another man's city. She is recovered the way a holding is recovered, by a war fought over her. And at the end the question asked of her is whether she is still sound — whether the good came back unspoiled. That is the language of property, of a thing whose worth turns on its condition, and it is the default the society reaches for, because it is the only category the order has for a value this high: a holding, to be taken, kept, contested, and appraised.

Her choices, in the immediate, refuse the category at every point. In the grove she sets a blade of grass on the ground between herself and Ravana and speaks past him — a holding asserting a jurisdiction the possession does not reach. He has her body in his city; she draws the one line his acquisition cannot cross, the line of her own person. Offered removal by Hanuman — the quiet re-taking of the holding by the same stealth that took it — she declines. Her reasons are her own and not ours: she will not willingly touch another man's body, and Rama's glory requires that he come and take her back himself. Neither is a claim about her autonomy in the terms we would reach for, and it is worth not converting them into one. But both do the same work against the category. A good does not decline the manner of its own repossession; a good has no view about whose act the recovery should be. She has one, and states it, and the recovery waits on it. And when the society is finally poised to appraise her, she seizes the appraisal and makes it her own act; she orders the fire herself. The thing being valued takes over the valuing. At each turn the acquisition refuses to behave as an acquisition, and society, defaulting to the only category it has, keeps reaching for her as a holding anyway.

And there is a second thing at the fire, on Rama's side, and here the reading has to be built carefully, because there are two speeches and they do not say the same thing.

To the gods, afterward, he says it plainly: as a man he knew her mind had never left him, and the proving was not warranted by anything he privately doubted; he let it happen for the sake of the three worlds. Taken alone, that is the distinction this thread has been tracing — the private knowledge real, the public establishment necessary, neither swallowing the other. It is the inverse of Dasharatha, who let the private promise become the public act and broke the state, and the refusal of Jabali, who would have dissolved the private entirely.

But that is his account of the scene, given later, and it is not what he says in the scene. There he is not detached at all. He tells her he fought the war to clear the blemish on his lineage, that he has no attachment to her, and that she may turn her mind to Lakshmana, Bharata, Sugriva, or Vibhishana. That is possession and rage, said aloud, to her, in front of everyone.

Both speeches are in the text. The jurisdictional reading accounts for the first and does not account for the second, and this essay is not going to make the second disappear to keep the first clean. What can be said is that the two frames are held apart in his own later telling, and that in the moment itself something else came through as well — and that a reading which found only the composure would be describing a man who is not on the page.

That is the immediate layer, and Rama's holding of it. Under both runs the longer one, which is the reading finding its outer limit. Concentration works on gold, on tribute, on anything that is fully itself when held. It does not work on her, and the reason is not moral but structural: the value Ravana reached for is a value that is only present when it is free. He can seal her body behind the water and cannot touch the thing that made her worth taking, because that thing — her own person, her heart, which as she says was the part that was in her power — is exactly what withdraws under holding. The more completely she is possessed, the more completely the value is absent. This is why she alone, of everything ever drawn across that water, emits. A held person is a beacon precisely because a person held against her own heart is not actually held; the unpossessed part is what calls out. The beacon is the value refusing to be concentrated.

So Sita shows what the wealth axis cannot do, at the far end of the direction the essay before this one traced. Concentration past a threshold must convert to reach, or plateau — that is the axis met on its own ground. But there is a class of value it cannot hold at all, because holding it is what makes it vanish, and the highest values are all of that class: a person's devotion, their consent, the heart that is only given and never taken. Ravana's error was not that he wanted, and not that he took. It was that he applied the logic of the holding to the one kind of good the logic destroys in the act of acquiring. Everything else in his hoard was still there when he sealed it. She was not — and the war that followed was the value he could not hold calling the world to the place he had sealed it.

## Vali, and the offer his wife brings him

The clearest single instance of the mechanism is Vali, because the offer comes from the person closest to him, at the moment it would have saved him, and he lets it pass — and the epic lets us watch him do it.

Rama has allied with Sugriva, Vali's deposed brother, and the alliance runs through a killing: Rama kills Vali with an arrow from cover, in a duel he is not part of, and names as his ground a prescribed transgression — Vali went to his brother's wife, a line the order draws and does not blur.

But before the arrow, the offer. Sugriva challenges Vali a second time, and Tara — Vali's wife, who reads the situation better than he does — warns him. She has learned the Kosala princes are in the area. She brings him the context. And watch exactly what Vali does with it, because it is the whole mechanism in one exchange. He does not dispute her facts. He reasons from them — and reasons truly, as far as he goes: *Rama knows dharma, he is grateful, why would he do something wicked to me?* Every word of that is sound. What Vali cannot do is read his own position into it. He applies his reading to Rama's character and never turns it on himself, where a transgression is sitting in plain view. Tara surfaces the wider view. Vali takes half of it — the half about Rama — and cannot take the half about himself, because seeing his own exposure is the one extension the default will not make.

That is why, when Rama names the transgression, Vali agrees at once and without resistance. The agreement is not a beaten man submitting. It is a man being shown the thing he could not surface on his own — the part of the context Tara's offer contained and he could not take in. And notice the register of what he could not take. Vali's every objection is personal — *you* had no quarrel with *me*, *I* would have helped *you*, we had no feud between us. He reads the whole thing as one man's grievance against another. Rama does not answer on that ground at all. He does not say he needed Sugriva, and he does not say he was wronged; he answers only in the register of the order — a prescribed transgression, a standing, a boundary the realm draws. He keeps it public and declines the personal frame entirely. And Vali, dying, makes every objection a reader would make, and then sets them down: it is exactly as you have said; I spoke before in confusion; your words conform to dharma; protect me. Read one way, that is Vali being brought from the personal frame to the public one — the wider view he had declined from his living wife, taken at last from the arrow.

And the retraction is not isolated testimony from a beaten man, which is the objection that comes first and does not survive the text. Tara had gone to Vali before the fight, having heard from Angada whom Sugriva had allied with. She named Rama, weighed him, and told her husband plainly not to take the field — make peace with your brother, seek this man's friendship, do not draw his enmity. Vali dismissed her, and mark the ground he dismissed her on: Rama is dharmatma, a man who knows dharma and does not act unrighteously, and so there is nothing to fear from him. That is Vali in full health, unthreatened, staking his life on Rama's dharmic judgement. The dying words agree with the living ones. He is not discovering a new opinion under an arrow; he is repeating one he held when he had nothing to lose by holding otherwise.

What stays open is narrower and it does not close. Rama's stated basis is that the land is Ikshvaku land and Bharata holds the sovereignty and he acts under it — which is a derivation, not a claim from nowhere, and it sits exactly with the arrangement Bharata built: legitimacy in one place, administration in another, and the enforcement running off the first. But whether Ikshvaku writ ever ran in Kishkindha, nobody in the scene establishes. Tara does not say it does; she says the man is dangerous. Vali does not say it does; he says the man is righteous. A paramount power's reach is precisely the thing everyone assumes and nobody tests until it costs them something.

So what the order cannot settle from inside itself is whether the killing should be credited — whether an enforcement carried out from cover, by a hand that stood to gain, on a reach nobody in the scene puts to the test, is one you accept. That is not the epic failing to answer. It is that there is no seat above the account from which the answer could come. The epic hands you the killing entire — the charge, the concealment, the gain, and the clean equation the order draws over all three — and leaves the crediting to you. Which is the mechanism once more, turned on the reader: the wider view offered, and the taking left in your hands.

And now set Tara beside Manthara, because together they expose the mechanism completely. They are the same function pointed in opposite directions. Manthara surfaces a narrowing view and it is taken. Tara surfaces a widening one and it is declined. Manthara needs no power and succeeds; Tara has every reason and love and fails. And the reason is not that one woman is persuasive and the other weak. It is direction. Manthara pushes with the grain — she lifts a subdued default into view, and lifting it is enough. Tara pushes against it — she asks Vali to hold his own exposure in sight, which is the one thing the default resists. The narrowing offer is easy to take because it is already there. The widening offer is hard to take because it asks the person past where they are. Kaikeyi and Vali are the same disposition seen twice: take what is surfaced when it runs with you, let it pass when it runs against you.

And neither woman holds any power at all. That is the part worth keeping. It costs nothing to surface a thing for someone — no standing, no force, no claim on them. It takes only being beside them and saying what is there. Which is why the mechanism is everywhere in this story, and why it can turn a kingdom either way.

## Those who read it through

Two figures read their context all the way to its end and act on it to its absolute consequence, and they are worth holding because they show the extension taken, not just declined.

Vibhishana sees his own order's trajectory — where Ravana's reading ends, and that it ends in ruin — and does the thing almost no one in the epic does: he acts on the wider view against every bond that holds him. He crosses to the other side, before any bridge exists, alone, over the same water only his kind can cross. His whole council tells him the defector will be received as a trap, and Rama takes him in — because a reading from inside the sealed place is the one thing the periphery cannot produce for itself, and it has just walked out on its own. Vibhishana is Ravana's brother offered the same context Ravana was offered, and taking the extension Ravana refused. The two of them are the cleanest matched pair in the story after Manthara and Tara: the same sight available to both, and one reads to the end of it and one stops where it costs him.

And Sugriva, once the alliance is struck and the search is his to run, does the thing the mainland had never done. He does not answer a concentrated force with a stronger one. He spreads the search as wide and thin as the map allows — thousands of ordinary creatures whose whole way of life is going and looking, sent to every direction. What surfaces inside that dispersal is the point. Hanuman sits on the southern shore with the rest of them. As a child he went three hundred yojanas straight up in one leap, at the rising sun, mistaking it for fruit. The water in front of him is a hundred across. The epic gives no reason why he does not move. It only shows Jambavan standing in front of him and reciting it back: your father, your birth, that leap, the boons it won you, now prepare.

And Jambavan, doing it, says of himself that he is aged now and his valour decayed, that among them all the qualities are Hanuman's. He is Manthara and Tara again in a third form — the one who surfaces the capacity is the one with none of his own left. Every load-bearing capacity in the search is like that: sitting where it sits, unacted on, brought into use by someone alongside naming it. Under Vali none of it had surfaced, though the same creatures lived in his kingdom, because an apex does not ask, and the unasked do not disclose.

## The one to whom it is always offered

And now Rama, who is not what the default reading makes him — not the good man among the wicked — but something the mechanism makes precise. He is the one to whom the wider view is offered at every turn, and who takes it.

Trace it. In the emptied hut he reaches for the worst instrument he holds — he speaks of unmaking creation, force at its maximum brought down on everything — and Lakshmana surfaces the wider view, and Rama takes it and lowers it. At the shore he reaches for it again, the strung bow, the sea about to be burned, and again the wider view is offered — the ocean cannot stop being a sea on command, but there is a builder in your own host — and again he takes it, and the bridge gets built where the astra would have accomplished nothing. Kabandha reasons with him and he goes. Vibhishana's council is doubted by everyone and Rama takes him in. Tara's counsel, Jambavan's, Sampati's word, the ocean's — the offer arrives from every side, and it keeps landing.

That is what to see in him, and it is not a higher faculty. He is triggered exactly as everyone else is — offered, counseled, corrected. He does not see further unaided. He is dissuaded, and takes the dissuasion; informed, and takes the information; corrected, and takes the correction. He does not treat a correction as an injury to his sovereignty. Where Ravana declines the offer and Vali cannot take it in, Rama lets it revise him.

And it is worth marking what kind of offer the arrow that killed Vali was, because it revises the pattern rather than completing it.

It was not unoffered. Sugriva asked for it; it is the explicit term of the alliance, promised before the search begins. So this is not the one place Rama acts alone. It is the one place the offer comes from the party who stands to gain by it, and points inward rather than outward — and he takes it as readily as he takes the others.

Which means the record does not say he is the man who takes the widening offer. It says he is the man who takes the offer in front of him. Nearly all the offers around him widen — Lakshmana's, the ocean's, Kabandha's, Tara's kind — and one did not, and he did not sort them. That is a smaller claim than the one this section has been building, and it is closer to what the epic shows. It may also be why this is the act the tradition has never been able to leave alone.

Lakshmana is the counter-case that completes him. At the hut, offered nothing wider — only Sita's fear, and an accusation his devotion cannot stand beneath — he lets the personal reading override the post he was set to hold, and leaves, and the house opens. He is not offered a widening view there; he is pushed by a narrowing one, the accusation that surfaces his need to not be thought uncaring, and he takes it the way Kaikeyi took Manthara's. Same man who elsewhere holds firm, undone at the one moment a narrowing offer runs with his grain. Even Rama's closest reader is, at that door, another chooser going with what was surfaced.

## The reading we bring

Here is what was laid out, once the sorting is set down.

Every figure in the core does the one thing the epic admits — reads a context and chooses on it. What binds them arrives from inside the same world, in human words that can be argued with, and never from a seat above the situation. And because the default reads a context only as far as it reads itself, the wider view always arrives as an offer, from someone alongside, and the whole of the drama is the taking or the letting-pass. Manthara surfaces a default and Kaikeyi takes it. Tara surfaces an extension and Vali lets it pass. Ravana is offered the wider view again and again and declines it every time. Vibhishana is offered the same and reads it to the end and crosses. Rama is offered it at every turn and takes it — the widening ones, mostly, and one that was not.

Nobody here is good or wicked. They are depths of a single act, and the depth is set by which offers a person can take in.

And it should be said, before that sentence is allowed to stand as a resolution, that it is not as neutral as it sounds. Depth is a valuation. To say that Ravana stayed with the reading that served him, and that Rama extended to what the jurisdiction required, is to have ranked them — on a different axis than the moral reading uses, but ranked them all the same. This essay has not dissolved the sorting. It has changed the currency it sorts in, from virtue to sight, and there is a real question, which it does not answer, about whether that is an improvement or only a relocation. Failing to see is not obviously more forgivable than failing to be good; it may only be less answerable.

And now turn it where it has been pointing the whole time. You came to this epic with a reading already installed, and it is the moral one, and it is your default exactly as concentration was Ravana's and honour was Vali's. It fires before you have looked. It sorts the cast into the wicked and the good — Kaikeyi, Vali, Ravana on one side, Rama on the other — and having sorted them, it stops, satisfied, before the other readings are ever reached. It does not get the cast wrong, quite. Kaikeyi's act has a moral surface; so does Ravana's. The default reading is not false. It is only first, and it is only shallow, and it closes the question at the first answer the way every default does. That is what a default is: the reading that runs with your grain, surfaced the moment you open the book, taken before you have chosen to take it.

The epic has been offering you the wider view the whole time. That is what the staging is — Tara counseling Vali where you can watch the extension declined, Ravana refusing his brother where you can see the road not taken, the whole cast arranged so that the offer and the letting-pass are visible to a reader even when they were invisible to the one inside. The discussion is triggered, in you, at every turn. And mostly it is not taken, because the moral reading has already answered, and a question that feels answered is not reopened.

This essay is not asking you to give up the moral reading. It is your default and it is nearly everyone's and it is not wrong. It is only asking you to notice that it is a default — that it surfaced before you chose it, that it ran with your grain, that it closed the question early — and to see that in noticing this you are being offered exactly what everyone in the story was offered: a wider view, from alongside, that you are free to take in or let pass. Ravana let it pass. Vali let it pass. Vibhishana took it and crossed. The offer is the same. What you do with it is not the epic's to decide, and it was never anyone's but the chooser's, and here, at the last, the chooser is you.

The next part of this exploration picks up the second of the two pressures, and holds it against the other epic. The contrast is what makes the holding worth doing.
